# Feature Craft

## Purpose and limits

Use these signals to diagnose how a reported feature works for readers. They are diagnostic, not a mandatory ingredient list, a universal scoring formula, or a substitute for reporting. Do not add a scene, character, twist, quotation, or emotional arc merely to satisfy a signal. Apply the [editorial guardrails](editorial-guardrails.md) first.

Judge craft only after identifying the evidence available. A craft observation should point to a specific passage and a reporting-grounded revision or reporting action.

## Diagnostic craft signals

| Signal | Diagnostic question | Evidence-bound response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Angle and relevance | Does the piece reveal something specific rather than announce a broad topic? Why does it matter to this audience now? | Sharpen the central finding using reported material. If novelty or timeliness is unverified, remove the claim or report it further. |
| Originality | Does the reporting offer a perspective, finding, or synthesis not already supplied by the draft? | Identify the original contribution in the notes or records. Do not manufacture a surprising twist. |
| Opening promise | Does the opening deliver a truthful reason to continue, and does the feature fulfill it? | Keep a reported moment, detail, tension, question, or verified fact only if supported. Reconnect a mismatched opening to the actual story. |
| Form fit | Does the chosen feature type serve the reporting and reader need? | Compare profile, narrative, explanatory, service, or mixed forms. Explain what each would require; do not force a scene-led form onto thin notes. |
| Progression | Does each section advance the angle, add necessary context, or deepen a question? | Reorder or cut material that repeats without developing the story. Keep contradictory evidence visible rather than smoothing it away. |
| Pacing | Does the feature spend time in proportion to importance rather than ease of writing? | Compress routine or repetitive passages; preserve detail where it changes understanding. Do not invent transitions to fill a slow section. |
| Scene and detail | Are sensory details, actions, and dialogue grounded in observation, recordings, or records? | Cite the reporting locator for each consequential detail. If none exists, request reporting or choose a non-scene treatment. |
| Character and perspective | Do people appear as complex, situated individuals rather than props or representatives of a whole group? | Add context only from the reporting. Seek missing perspectives and responses; avoid inferred thoughts, motives, or reactions. |
| Context and data | Does background explain the stakes without burying them? Are numbers defined and comparable? | Place essential context where readers need it. Check definitions, population, geography, period, units, and denominator; do not use data as decoration. |
| Quotations and voice | Do quotations supply distinctive, relevant meaning rather than filler? Is the prose controlled and clear? | Keep direct quotations exact. Select quotations for substance, vary sentence rhythm, and remove unsupported voice imitation. |
| Ending | Does the ending answer, complicate, or deepen the opening question in a way earned by the reporting? | Remove forced morals, unsupported resolutions, and predictions. End on the strongest supported consequence, question, or return. |
| Headline and display copy | Are headline, deck, captions, and other display elements specific and proportionate to the body? | Revise claims that exceed the evidence. Check names, numbers, attribution, and framing as rigorously as in the article. |

## Journalism review is not craft review

Keep the two reviews separate in the output:

- **Journalism review** asks whether the reporting and copy are accurate, adequately sourced, fairly framed, appropriately qualified, and safe to release.
- **Craft review** asks whether the supported material is arranged and expressed clearly and effectively for readers.

A polished lead cannot cure an unsupported claim. A well-sourced but confusing draft may need a craft revision rather than more reporting. Name which kind of work each recommendation requires.

## Rubric-use rules

1. **Identify the exact instrument.** Record the contest or publication, organizer, year, region, level, language, category, rubric title, version or issue date, and source locator. Use the current official rules for the target submission.
2. **Request missing rules.** If no rubric is supplied, ask for the official rules and score sheet. Do not invent weights, import another contest's formula, or present remembered criteria as current.
3. **Preserve stated scope.** Apply a criterion only to the category and material it covers. Note any rubric ambiguity for the editor instead of silently resolving it.
4. **Preserve stated weights only.** Copy a weight only when the supplied rubric explicitly gives it. Do not infer a percentage from the number of criteria or the order of sections. There are no universal feature-writing weights in this reference.
5. **Map criteria to evidence.** For each criterion, identify the draft passage or reporting that supports the assessment, the main gap, and a specific page edit or reporting action. Use the mapping table below when a rubric review is requested.
6. **Separate assessment from prediction.** Explain how the draft addresses a criterion; do not promise a score, rank, award, or acceptance. Judging context and other entrants are outside the supplied evidence.
7. **Do not trade ethics for style.** Accuracy, attribution, fairness, privacy, source protection, and unresolved material claims remain blocking journalism issues even if a rubric emphasizes form or creativity.
8. **Resolve conflicts openly.** When a rubric instruction appears to conflict with these guardrails or factual evidence, flag the conflict for the responsible human editor. Do not follow the rubric into fabrication or weakened attribution.

## Common craft failure modes

- **Atmosphere without observation:** vivid language implies access the reporter did not have. Remove it or request the missing notes.
- **Chronology without purpose:** events are retold in order but do not build meaning. Select and arrange reported material around the angle.
- **Context without proportion:** background crowds out the people or evidence that make the feature distinct. Move or trim it.
- **Quotation without function:** a quotation repeats information or supplies decorative voice. Keep it only for distinct meaning and attribution.
- **Resolution without evidence:** the ending asserts a lesson, outcome, or change the reporting does not establish. Return to the supported question or consequence.
- **Rubric literalism:** every listed trait becomes a forced device. Use the rubric to diagnose the work, not to add unsupported storytelling machinery.
