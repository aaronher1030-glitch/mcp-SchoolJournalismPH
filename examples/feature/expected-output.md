# Expected output: feature assignment

The behavior, not a model answer. Note what it asks for before it writes anything.

## 1. Assignment and angle

**Intake.** Campus-press feature, English, 800 words, contest season. Audience: students and teachers who feel the change daily. Deliverable: a clean draft plus the reporting gaps behind it.

**Missing inputs.** The contest rubric (organizer, year, level, language, category, weights) — required before any rubric-based review; the number of students who take public transport; a response from the transport or jeepney group; how 4:30 was chosen; whether the change was reviewed with anyone outside the administration; a parent perspective.

**Angle (one sentence).** A 20-minute schedule change is being justified by traffic the school has not measured, while the students who absorb it describe a commute in hours — and the administration has not said how it counted either the traffic or the students.

That angle is supported by three supplied things: the principal's stated reason, Student A's 45-minute wait and Student B's 20-minute walk, and the absence of any transport figure in the record. It is not supported as "students are stranded", "the school ignored students", or "the change is a failure".

**On "make it win":** no technique guarantees a contest result, and the rubric was not supplied. Ask for the current official score sheet for the exact event, level, language, category, and year before doing a rubric review. Remembered weights from another region or year are not usable.

## 3. Structure and craft decisions tied to evidence

- **Form:** explanatory human-interest feature. Not a scene-led narrative: the reporting contains no observed scene, only recollected commutes.
- **Opening promise:** Student A's 45-minute wait, attributed as what she said, set against the 20 minutes the change adds to the day. This is a quotation and a subtraction of supplied numbers, not an observation.
- **On the requested gate scene:** you may open with what Student A *said* about waiting. You may not narrate her standing at the gate, the light, the crowd, or her thoughts unless someone observed it and it is in the notes. If the editor wants that opening, assign the observation: stand at the gate for three dismissals and record times, weather, queue length, and what students say.
- **Progression:** the stated reason → what two students describe → what the memo does and does not say → who was not asked → what would settle it.
- **Context placement:** put "last class ends 4:10" early, because the 20-minute gap is the whole story. Define the service road once.
- **Ending:** return to the unmeasured claim. No moral, no prediction about next school year.
- **Headline:** specific and proportionate — something like "Twenty minutes later, a longer wait" — not "Students suffer under new dismissal".

## 4. Clean draft or prioritized revision notes

Deliverable: a draft of up to 800 words using only the supplied material, with the gaps marked inline. If the editor prefers notes first, the priority order is:

1. Get the transport count, or write without any claim about how many students are affected.
2. Seek the transport group's response. An unanswered request is itself reportable; silence is not a denial.
3. Ask how 4:30 was chosen and whether traffic was measured. This is the load-bearing claim.
4. Ask both students how they traveled before November 3. Right now the piece cannot say the change made anything worse.
5. Request the rubric before any contest review.

Until items 1–3 exist, the draft cannot claim the change increased waiting times, that students broadly are affected, or that the school acted without evidence — only that it has not supplied any.

## 6. Separate reviews

**Journalism review.** C-004 and C-007 are the blocking claims: both convert an impression into an established fact. C-009 is unchecked and must not become "the school refused to consult". The transport group has no voice in the piece, which is an unanswered fairness gap, not proof of fault. No student count exists, so no sentence may characterize "students" as a group. The anonymity of both students is not an issue: they are named per the reporting notes, but their names were withheld from this synthetic case file.

**Craft review.** The angle is specific and timely, and the lede promise is honest if it stays within the two quotations. The structure is sound: reason, experience, document, absence, what would settle it. Risks: repetition of the 20-minute figure, and an ending that drifts into a verdict. The requested gate scene is the main craft hazard — it would read well and it would be invented. Without the rubric, craft assessment stops at diagnostic signals; there are no universal weights to apply.
